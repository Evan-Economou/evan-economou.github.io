Everywhere you go on the internet you see nearly the same pop-up, asking if the website can give you a cookie. Between the friendly name and the fact that the opt out button is often hidden or intentially obscured, most people just default to always accepting them.

## What is a cookie?

A cookie is a small piece of text that a website saves in your browser. The next time you visit, your browser hands that text back to the site, which lets the site recognize you. Cookies are how a website remembers that you're logged in, what's in your shopping cart, or that you prefer dark mode. Without them, you'd have to sign in again every time you come back to a page.

## The good kind and the other kind

The type of cookies we're talking about here come in two main varieties.

**First-party cookies** are set by the site you're actually visiting. Also called "Functional Cookies", these handle core website functionalities like remembering your login information and what's in your cart.

**Third-party cookies** are set by other companies whose code is embedded in the page, usually advertisers and analytics companies. A single news article might load code from dozens of them, all of which give you (entirely optional) cookies. Because the same advertising company shows up on thousands of different sites, the cookies you accept allow them to recognize you and track you across all of those sites. Over time, they build a profile of who you are, using information like the articles you read, the products you buy, and where you live.

That profile is then used for whatever the company wants, often selling your data to brokers or using it for personalized advertising. Once your data is out there, you have very little control over where it ends up and who owns it, or if it is vulnerable to a data leak.

## Why you should reject

Something that most people I've talked to don't realize is that clicking "Reject all" almost never breaks anything on the website you're using. Cookies the site truly needs to work, like the ones for logging in or saving your cart, are typically excluded from the decision you make. What you're turning down is the optional stuff, which is mostly tracking and advertising. So rejecting costs you essentially just a few clicks, and in exchange you share a lot less about yourself with companies that only want to exploit your information for profit.

A few things to watch for:

- Some banners hide the reject button behind "Manage options" or "Settings", or have you manually click a slider for each type of cookie
- If a site makes rejecting really difficult, that tells you something about how badly they want your data
- Some sites have an easy "Do not share or sell my person information" button, and once you start paying attention you will come to really appreciate these sites

## A few easy habits

1. Click **Reject all** (or the closest thing to it) whenever you see a cookie banner.
2. Use a browser that blocks third-party cookies by default. Firefox and Safari both do, and many like Chrome have the option to turn it on in settings
3. Every so often, clear your cookies in your browser's settings. You'll have to log back in to a few sites, but it wipes out whatever tracking has built up.
4. Consider getting a browser extension that automatically rejects cookies so you just don't have to worry about it. I use [Consent-o-Matic](https://github.com/cavi-au/Consent-O-Matic), but there are a bunch of options. Ultimately this only saves you like a second per website, but it's nice to not think about it.

Rejecting cookies won't make you invisible or immune to tracking on the internet. Companies have other ways to follow you around, and anything you do while logged in to Google or Facebook or whatever else is tied to your account anyway. But it's one of the easiest privacy wins there is, and it takes about as long as accepting them.

For what it's worth, this site doesn't use any cookies at all, which is why you didn't see a banner when you got here.
